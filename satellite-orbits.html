Why Do Satellite Orbits Matter?

Remote sensing satellites travel around Earth in specific orbits. The orbit affects what a satellite can observe, how often it can observe the same location, and the type of Earth observation it can perform.

Key idea: Different orbits are useful for different remote sensing purposes.

Low Earth Orbit — LEO

Low Earth Orbit satellites travel relatively close to Earth's surface. Because they are close to Earth, they can be useful for detailed observations and imaging.

Typical characteristics

  • Relatively low altitude
  • Shorter orbital period
  • Often used for Earth observation
  • Can provide relatively detailed imagery
Example: Many Earth-observing satellites operate in low Earth orbits.

Medium Earth Orbit — MEO

Medium Earth Orbit satellites travel farther from Earth than LEO satellites. MEO is commonly associated with navigation satellite systems.

  • Higher altitude than LEO
  • Longer orbital period than LEO
  • Larger area can be covered from greater altitude

High Earth Orbit

High Earth orbits are much farther from Earth's surface. A satellite at a greater altitude can observe a very large portion of Earth.

As orbital altitude increases, the orbital period generally increases.

Polar Orbit

A polar satellite travels approximately over Earth's polar regions. As Earth rotates beneath the satellite, repeated passes can allow the satellite to observe different parts of Earth's surface.

Why is this useful?

Polar or near-polar orbits are useful for observing large portions of Earth over repeated passes.

Geostationary Orbit

A geostationary satellite appears to remain above approximately the same location on Earth's surface.

This is possible when the satellite's orbital period matches Earth's rotation and the orbit has the appropriate orientation.

Remote sensing connection: A satellite that continuously observes the same broad region can be useful for monitoring changes over time.

Geosynchronous Orbit

A geosynchronous satellite has an orbital period equal to Earth's rotation period. Its ground track does not necessarily remain over exactly the same location.

A geostationary orbit is a special type of geosynchronous orbit.

Kepler's Third Law

Kepler's Third Law describes the relationship between a satellite's orbital period and the size of its orbit.

For circular-orbit comparisons, the important idea is that satellites farther from Earth take longer to complete an orbit.

Remember: Larger orbit → longer orbital period.
