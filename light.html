Light & Remote Sensing

Science Olympiad Division B — Remote Sensing

Season 2026–2027

What Is Light?

Light is electromagnetic radiation that can carry energy from one place to another. Remote sensing instruments use electromagnetic radiation to collect information about Earth's surface and atmosphere.

Different wavelengths interact with Earth's materials in different ways. By measuring these interactions, scientists can learn about vegetation, water, soil, buildings, temperature, and other features.

Remember:

Remote sensing depends on how electromagnetic radiation interacts with objects and how sensors detect that radiation.

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum contains different types of electromagnetic radiation. They are arranged according to wavelength, frequency, and energy.

  • Radio waves
  • Microwaves
  • Infrared
  • Visible light
  • Ultraviolet
  • X-rays
  • Gamma rays

In remote sensing, different parts of the electromagnetic spectrum are useful for different applications.

Key idea:

Shorter wavelength → higher frequency → higher energy.

Longer wavelength → lower frequency → lower energy.

Reflection

Reflection occurs when electromagnetic radiation strikes a surface and is sent back from that surface.

Different materials reflect different amounts of radiation at different wavelengths. This difference in reflectance can help remote sensing systems distinguish between objects and land-cover types.

Example:

Vegetation reflects strongly in near-infrared wavelengths. This makes infrared measurements useful for studying vegetation.

Refraction

Refraction is the change in direction of a wave when it moves from one medium into another medium and its speed changes.

The amount of bending depends on the properties of the materials involved.

Absorption

Absorption occurs when a material takes in electromagnetic radiation rather than reflecting or transmitting all of it.

Different materials absorb different wavelengths. These absorption patterns provide useful information for remote sensing.

Example:

Atmospheric gases can absorb particular wavelengths of electromagnetic radiation. This affects which wavelengths can be detected from satellites.

Scattering

Scattering occurs when electromagnetic radiation is redirected by particles or molecules.

Particles in the atmosphere can scatter radiation and affect the quality of remotely sensed data.

Remember:

Atmospheric scattering can make it harder for a sensor to measure the radiation coming from Earth's surface.

Wien's Displacement Law

Wien's Displacement Law relates the temperature of an object to the wavelength at which it emits radiation most strongly.

λmax = b / T

Where:

  • λmax = wavelength of maximum emission
  • b = Wien's displacement constant
  • T = absolute temperature in Kelvin
Important relationship:

As temperature increases, the wavelength of maximum emission decreases.

Wave Equation

The wave equation relates the speed, wavelength, and frequency of a wave.

c = λν

Where:

  • c = speed of light
  • λ = wavelength
  • ν = frequency
Remember:

For electromagnetic waves in a vacuum, the speed of light is constant. Therefore, wavelength and frequency have an inverse relationship.

Snell's Law

Snell's Law describes how a wave changes direction when it passes between two different materials.

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

Where:

  • n₁ = refractive index of the first material
  • n₂ = refractive index of the second material
  • θ₁ = angle of incidence
  • θ₂ = angle of refraction
Think about it:

When light enters a different medium, its speed changes. This can cause the direction of the light ray to change.

Inverse-Square Law for Irradiance

The inverse-square relationship describes how irradiance changes as the distance from a source changes.

I ∝ 1 / r²

As the distance from the source increases, irradiance decreases with the square of the distance.

Example:

If the distance from a source doubles, the irradiance becomes one-fourth as large, assuming the other conditions remain the same.

Stefan-Boltzmann Law

The Stefan-Boltzmann Law relates the thermal radiation emitted by an object to its absolute temperature.

E = σT⁴

Where:

  • E = emitted radiant energy per unit area
  • σ = Stefan-Boltzmann constant
  • T = absolute temperature in Kelvin
Important:

Because temperature is raised to the fourth power, a change in temperature can produce a much larger change in emitted radiation.

Science Olympiad Quick Review

  • Reflection = radiation bounces from a surface.
  • Refraction = radiation changes direction when entering another medium.
  • Absorption = radiation is taken in by a material.
  • Scattering = radiation is redirected by particles or molecules.
  • Wien's Law connects temperature and peak wavelength.
  • The wave equation connects wavelength and frequency.
  • Snell's Law describes refraction.
  • Inverse-square law describes how irradiance changes with distance.
  • Stefan-Boltzmann Law connects thermal emission with temperature.
