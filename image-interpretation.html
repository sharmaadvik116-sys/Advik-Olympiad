Image Interpretation

Science Olympiad Division B — Remote Sensing

Season 2026–2027

What Is Image Interpretation?

Image interpretation means examining remote sensing imagery and data to identify patterns, features, materials, and changes on Earth's surface.

Key idea: Interpret the evidence shown by the image rather than guessing from color alone.

Natural Color Imagery

Natural-color imagery uses visible wavelengths in a way that produces colors similar to what human vision normally sees.

What can you look for?

  • Water bodies
  • Vegetation
  • Buildings
  • Roads
  • Other visible surface features
Remember: Natural-color imagery is designed to look relatively familiar to human vision.

False Color Imagery

False-color displays assign selected wavelength information to display colors that may not match natural human vision.

False-color displays can make particular features easier to distinguish.

Important: A color in a false-color image does not necessarily represent the actual color of the object.

Grayscale Imagery

Grayscale imagery represents measurements using different shades from dark to light.

The meaning of brightness depends on the sensor and the data being displayed.

Example: A brighter area may represent a stronger measured signal, but you must know what the particular image represents before interpreting brightness.

Thermal Displays

Thermal imagery represents information related to thermal infrared radiation and surface temperature.

Different surfaces can have different thermal characteristics. Comparing thermal information can therefore help identify differences between materials or areas.

Remember: Thermal imagery is related to emitted thermal radiation, not simply the visible color of an object.

Radar Displays

Radar imagery is produced from active sensing. Radar instruments transmit energy and measure the response from Earth's surface.

Radar backscatter depends on properties of the surface and how the radar signal interacts with it.

Things that can affect radar observations

  • Surface characteristics
  • Surface roughness
  • Moisture
  • Geometry and viewing direction

Elevation Displays

Elevation products can show differences in terrain height. These products can help reveal mountains, valleys, slopes, and other changes in Earth's surface elevation.

Example: A Digital Elevation Model (DEM) can represent elevation values across a geographic area.

Contour Maps

Contour lines connect locations that have the same elevation.

Reading contour spacing

  • Closely spaced contours generally indicate a steeper slope.
  • Widely spaced contours generally indicate a gentler slope.
Important: Always check the contour interval before calculating elevation differences.

Classified Map Displays

A classified map assigns areas to categories based on their characteristics.

Examples of categories can include vegetation, water, agriculture, developed areas, or other land-cover types.

Important: Use the map legend to determine what each class or color means.

A Simple Image Interpretation Process

  1. Identify what type of image or map you are looking at.
  2. Check the date and location.
  3. Check the legend and scale.
  4. Identify the sensor or wavelength information when available.
  5. Look for patterns, shapes, textures, and brightness differences.
  6. Compare different areas of the image.
  7. Use the available evidence to identify the feature or change.

Practice Questions

1. What is the main difference between natural-color and false-color imagery?
2. Why can false-color imagery make some features easier to identify?
3. What type of sensing produces radar imagery?
4. What type of information can thermal imagery provide?
5. What do contour lines represent?
6. What does closely spaced contour information usually indicate about a slope?
7. Why should you check the legend before interpreting colors on a classified map?
